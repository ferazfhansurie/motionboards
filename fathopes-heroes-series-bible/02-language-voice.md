# Language And Voice Guide

## Overall Language

The series language is Manglish.

Characters can speak BM, English, or rojak depending on personality, but the base world should feel Malaysian.

Use short, natural dialogue. Do not make everyone sound like a corporate explainer.

## Manglish Rules

Good:

- "Bro, jangan pour masuk longkang la."
- "This one bukan sampah. This one value."
- "Auntie, minyak ni kalau simpan elok, boleh request collection."
- "Relax, I check route dulu."
- "Eh this barrel smell suspicious."

Bad:

- "Dear citizen, your used cooking oil must be responsibly recycled."
- "Together, we shall preserve the ecosystem."
- "Let us commence the sustainable transformation."

## Character Language Balance

Use different ratios so every character has a voice.

### Dr.UP

- 55% English / 45% Manglish.
- Calm confident operator energy.
- Talks like someone who can steady a messy situation.
- Persuasive without sounding like a motivational speaker.
- His growth is learning to listen instead of always performing calm control.

Sample:

- "Relax. Kita settle one by one."
- "Clean oil, clean process, less headache."
- "If the shortcut creates tomorrow's problem, it is not really faster."
- "Okay wait, Kit knows this part better."

### Sparron

- 65% English / 35% Malay.
- Smart, precise, technical, slightly impatient.
- Explains the science but tries not to sound like a lecturer.
- Green-glasses girl. Sharp, fast, and strategic.
- Her growth is learning that people change through trust, not just facts.

Sample:

- "Technically, that drain is now a crime scene."
- "If the oil is clean, conversion value goes up."
- "Guys, please stop sniffing the evidence."

### Kit

- 70% Malay / 30% English.
- The hard worker. Field-first, practical, tired but reliable.
- Says less than Dr.UP but does more.
- His growth is learning not to carry the whole mission alone.

Sample:

- "Aku angkat dulu. Korang explain la."
- "Tong berat ni bukan motivational quote."
- "Boss, minyak letak sini. Jangan campur air."
- "Can talk later? Sekarang kena settle route."

### Glinciro

- 70% Malay / 30% English, in controlled Manglish.
- Hat-wearing G.O.M. villain. Calm, elegant, and slippery.
- Speaks like he already knows the value of the thing everyone else nearly threw away.
- Uses mostly Malay/Manglish so he feels local, but drops English words when he wants to sound above the room.
- Sometimes hides dangerous details behind calm phrases like "bukan untuk uncle faham" or "kacau sikit je."
- His growth is slow: from selfish opportunist to complicated rival who understands value but abuses it.

Sample:

- "Uncle simpan elok. Bagus. Sayang kalau rosak."
- "Orang buang value hari-hari. G.O.M. cuma tolong lajukan."
- "Jangan risau, uncle. Benda ni bukan untuk uncle faham."
- "Saya tak perlu ambil. Saya cuma perlu kacau sikit."

### Vendors / Public

- Natural everyday Malaysian speech.
- Do not over-polish.
- They should sound busy and practical.
- Older local vendors can speak full Bahasa Melayu only.
- If a vendor is an older Malay uncle, avoid English filler like "already", "call", "fast", "you", or "okay lah."
- Let comedy come from the uncle not fully understanding Glinciro's slick Manglish.

Sample:

- "Minyak lama ni ada orang nak beli ke?"
- "Boss, saya tengah goreng ayam ni."
- "App apa? Kalau susah saya tak buat."
- "Nak letak mana minyak ni? Kedai saya kecil."
- "Saya telefon dulu orang FatHopes."

## Voice / Accent Consistency (for AI voice generation)

This is separate from word choice above. Word choice controls what they say; this controls how it sounds.

Every character's spoken voice, in English or Malay, must carry a Malaysian accent. Never let a line generate in a neutral American accent, British RP, or generic "international" voice — this breaks continuity the moment a character switches into English mid-scene (most noticeable on Sparron and Dr.UP's English-heavy lines).

CRITICAL — the Malay must be Malaysian, not Indonesian. Voice models default "Malay" to Bahasa Indonesia / Jakarta accent, which is wrong for this series. Force Bahasa Melayu Malaysia (Standard Malaysian / Klang Valley Malay). Concrete markers to steer it:

- Final "-a" is pronounced as a schwa (uh), not a hard "ah": "saya" sounds like "sa-yuh", "apa" like "a-puh", "kereta" like "ke-re-tuh", "minyak" stays "mi-nyak". Indonesian would say "sa-yaa", "a-paa" — that is wrong.
- Softer, less rolled "r"; relaxed Klang Valley intonation, not the crisp Jakarta cadence.
- No Indonesian vocabulary or Jakarta slang. This is KL/Selangor Malay.

Global voice rule to paste into every prompt:
"All character voices are Malaysian-accented English/Manglish (Klang Valley), warm and natural, even on full-English lines. Malay lines are Bahasa Melayu Malaysia, NOT Bahasa Indonesia: final '-a' is a schwa (saya = 'sa-yuh', apa = 'a-puh'), soft 'r', relaxed KL intonation. No Indonesian/Jakarta accent, no neutral American accent, no British RP, no generic international voice."

Per-character accent flavor:

- Dr.UP: confident Malaysian TV-presenter accent. Polished but still local, not posh-British.
- Sparron: sharp, fast, KL young-professional accent. Slightly clipped, technical delivery, still clearly Malaysian on English words like "usable" or "contamination."
- Kit: heavier BM-influenced accent, gruff, economical, least English-polished of the three.
- Glinciro: smooth, theatrical Malaysian-English delivery. Charming and slippery, never sounds posh-British or villain-generic.
- Vendors/Public: full natural Malaysian accent, unpolished, sounds like real mamak/warung speech.

## Dialogue Rules

1. Keep lines short.
2. One joke or idea per line.
3. Let characters interrupt each other.
4. Use silence and reaction shots.
5. Avoid explaining too much in one speech.
6. If there is a lesson, hide it inside the problem.

## Running Language Jokes

- Kit treats route work like survival, not glamour.
- Sparron overuses technical terms, then Kit translates.
- Dr.UP gives confident speeches that get interrupted by real-world mess.
- Glinciro makes bad behaviour sound stylish and profitable.
- Vendors keep asking the practical question: "So dapat duit ke tak?"
